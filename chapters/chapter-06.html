6) Twelve Months or Twenty-Four?
The First Martian Choice

The argument over how many months Mars should have is really an argument over what a month is for.

On Earth, we rarely need to ask. A month is roughly one twelfth of the year and roughly four weeks long. The first property makes it useful for locating ourselves within the annual cycle; the second makes it useful for everyday planning. Mars separates those meanings.

Divide a Martian year of about 668.6 sols into twelve and each part lasts roughly 56 sols, close to eight weeks. Divide it into twenty-four and each lasts roughly 28 sols, close to four. Both answers are tidy. They simply preserve different things.

Levitt's twelve-month calendar and Gangale's twenty-four-month Darian Calendar are therefore useful not because one must defeat the other, but because they expose the choice unusually clearly.

Two answers to the same problem

Levitt begins with the annual structure. His year contains twelve months: eight of 56 sols and four of 55, arranged in the repeating pattern 56–56–55. Three months make one 167-sol common-year quarter and six make half a year.

The attraction is annual legibility. Twelve positions create a familiar map of the year: Month 2 is near the beginning, Month 6 brings the first half to a close, and Month 9 places us three quarters of the way through. Three months still make a quarter and six still make half a year.

The cost is duration. A 56-sol month contains eight seven-sol weeks and lasts much closer to two Earth months than one. If an Earth-born resident hears that something happened “last month”, their inherited intuition will be wrong unless they have already learnt what a Martian month means.

Darian begins from the opposite end. If Mars has a year nearly twice as long as Earth's while retaining an almost Earth-length day, preserving something close to familiar month duration suggests roughly twice as many months. Darian therefore uses twenty-four, normally 28 sols long with some shortened to 27.

A 28-sol month is exactly four seven-sol weeks. That keeps recurring reviews, billing periods, maintenance intervals and other roughly monthly activities much closer to the duration people already associate with the word. The cost moves upward: a year now contains twenty-four months, a quarter contains six and half a year contains twelve. The unit retains something like its terrestrial duration while giving up its familiar annual frequency.

The underlying choice is straightforward:

Levitt preserves the month as an annual division. Darian preserves it as a human-scale duration.

Neither property is trivial, and Mars no longer lets one unit preserve both.

The arithmetic nearly contains both calendars

What makes Levitt and Darian particularly interesting is how closely their structures are related.

Take a 56-sol Levitt month:

56 = 28 + 28

Take a 55-sol month:

55 = 28 + 27

Split all twelve long months in this way and the Martian year naturally becomes twenty-four shorter periods of 27 or 28 sols. The two systems are therefore not based on opposing pieces of mathematics. They are different hierarchies built over closely related partitions of the same year.

Gangale identified this relationship in his historical analysis. Bisecting Levitt's 55- and 56-sol months produces a short-period pattern closely resembling the architecture from which Darian's twenty-four months are built.

That tells us where the disagreement really sits: not in the arithmetic, but in which scale deserves to be the month.

Levitt promotes the 55/56-sol interval to the primary named annual unit. Darian promotes the 27/28-sol interval instead. The other scale does not cease to exist mathematically; it simply carries less civil prominence.

Once the problem is framed this way, the word month is doing much of the argumentative work. Remove the label and the underlying structure becomes rather less controversial. Mars has approximately twenty-four four-week periods in its year, and those periods can be paired into twelve larger annual divisions.

The real design question is which scale should carry which function.

Orientation and cadence

The simplest distinction is between orientation and cadence.

Orientation asks:

Where am I in the year?

Cadence asks:

How long until we do this again?

Earth's month answers both questions tolerably well. Mars makes them diverge.

For annual orientation, twelve divisions have obvious advantages. Twelve is easily divided into halves, thirds, quarters and sixths. It gives the year a manageable number of positions and preserves familiar annual relationships without requiring users to navigate twenty-four primary labels.

A twelve-part structure also offers a modest advantage for an Earth-connected civilisation. Both planets can retain twelve top-level annual divisions even though those divisions last very different amounts of time. That does not make an Earth month and a Martian month equivalent, but it reduces one layer of structural translation.

The strongest case for twenty-four divisions is different. Intervals of around 28 sols sit naturally at the four-week scale. They are short enough to support recurring planning and administration without another subordinate unit, and a 28-sol period consists of four complete seven-sol weeks.

The convenience therefore appears in different places. Twelve puts more of it into the shape of the year; twenty-four puts more into the operating rhythm inside the year.

That is more useful than asking which number is inherently more intuitive. An Earth-born resident arranging a recurring medical review might find the 28-sol scale immediately comfortable, while the same person trying to understand how far through the Martian year they were might prefer twelve larger divisions. Someone born on Mars may eventually find either arrangement completely ordinary.

We cannot establish that preference by arithmetic. What we can establish is the function each structure provides.

The problem continues above the month

The same separation appears one level higher.

If Mars retains twelve months, three can still form one quarter. The annual meaning remains exact: one quarter is one fourth of the year. But that Martian quarter lasts about 167 sols, approaching twenty-four seven-sol weeks. An Earth quarter lasts about thirteen weeks.

Choosing twenty-four approximately four-week months does not remove the higher-order problem. Six such months still form one true quarter of the Martian year, and that quarter is still much longer than the operating period terrestrial institutions associate with Q1 or Q2.

This matters because it shows that the problem is not merely vocabulary. The Martian year is genuinely longer. Every true annual fraction is therefore longer in human elapsed terms, and no calendar can make one fourth of the Martian year last thirteen weeks.

Civil design can only decide whether annual fractions and familiar operating intervals should continue sharing names, or whether their functions should be represented separately.

That makes the twelve-versus-twenty-four debate more useful than it first appears. It is not really asking how many boxes should appear on a wall calendar. It is asking whether annual orientation and recurring operating cadence must belong to the same unit.

Earth bundles those functions because its astronomical ratios make the arrangement convenient. Mars does not offer the same convenience.

Once that is understood, twelve and twenty-four stop looking like rival answers to a simple counting problem. They become two legitimate scales inside the same physical year, each good at a different job.

The real choice is not twelve or twenty-four. It is whether one unit should still be expected to do both.
